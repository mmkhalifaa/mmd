What this is

A workshop kit, not a slide show. Clients play; the AI plays back.

The room
One iPad per table, four to six clients around it, one advisor. The app runs everything: the setup, the clock, the AI, the facilitator notes. A screen for the scoreboard when tables compete.
The engine
The AI plays characters, plays decisions forward, translates, values, mediates and explains. It never decides. Every AI moment has a written fallback, so the evening runs even without a connection.
The rule
Everything is fiction: the Valmonts, their company, their founder, their fraudster. Nothing about a real family goes into the iPad. First names, votes and answers are all the app keeps, and one tap clears it.
Why games

People remember what they did, not what they were told.

1
Put them in another seat
Five Seats and Dinner with the Founder. Argue the position you least agree with; hear a dead man disagree with you. Perspective cannot be lectured into a room.
2
Show the consequence without the cost
Three Generations, the Investment Committee, the Grant Round, the Buyout. Sixty years in thirty minutes; five years later in five seconds. The lesson lands because the money was theirs for an hour.
3
Show the gap
The Values Auction and The Call. What you say you value against what you would pay for; what you know about fraud against what you do at 16:31 on a Sunday.

How the app runs

Set the table once. Everything else is a tile.

Facilitator drawer
In every game: the point, the questions to ask, what to listen for, the timing. Restart a game or clear the table from here.
Demo mode
Sample AI output, no network. For rehearsals, and for the moment the Wi-Fi dies. If the AI cannot be reached, the app falls back on its own and says so.
Progress saved
Leave a game, come back after the break. Seats, votes, stamps and verdicts are kept until the table is cleared.
Across tables
Three Generations posts every table to one scoreboard. The Buyout pairs two tables. Everything else is one iPad, one table.
Five Seats
60 to 90 min
Take a seat that is not your own.

Five characters at one fictional family table. Everyone plays a seat that is not their real-life role, and argues it through five decisions a family cannot avoid.

The perspective game: how it flows
1
Take seats
Each player claims a seat that is not their own. Empty seats are played by the AI.
2
Read the brief
What the seat cares about, fears, and how it argues. A private brief, revealed by press-and-hold.
3
The situation
One scenario, five lenses on the same facts: governance, communication, entrepreneurship, education, succession.
4
At the table
Twelve minutes on the clock. A spin picks who opens. The table asks the AI to argue an empty seat, play the decision forward, or translate a message.
5
Decide
A vote, a rewritten message, a score, a plan, a path every seat can live with. The AI never votes.
6
Debrief and stamp
Each player names one thing they now understand about another seat. It goes in the passport.
The AI
Argues the empty seats, plays the decision forward to 2031, shows what each seat heard in a message, stress-tests an idea, builds a learning path, throws curveballs, and coaches any player as their seat.
The table
Argues its seat, not itself. Votes, writes, scores, decides.
Time
Five rounds of twelve minutes. Short version: rounds I, II and V in sixty minutes.
Five Seats, in play
What they leave with

What the other seat is afraid of, in its own words.

The distance between a message sent and a message heard.

A succession path the whole table can live with, and what it cost each seat.

The advisor's moment
"What did you hear tonight that you did not expect?" Then the stamp.
Watch for
People arguing themselves instead of their seat. Send them back to the brief.
Three Generations
30 min
Sixty years, five decisions a decade, two dials.

Every table inherits the same €200 million in 1995 and steers it to 2055. The money is one dial; the family is the other. The scoreboard decides.

The competitive game: how it flows
1
Open 1995
€200 million, trust 70 out of 100. Six decades ahead.
2
Turn the cards
Two family events a decade: a death without a will, a divorce, a rescue, a fraud, a tax reset, forty cousins.
3
Five decisions
Spend, invest, govern, educate, give. Each has a price, and each changes what the events cost.
4
Close the decade
The ledger: returns, spending, what each event took. The fortune counts up or down.
5
The chronicle
The AI writes three sentences for the family archive. Then the next decade opens.
6
2055
A verdict, a legacy score, and the scoreboard of every table in the room.
The AI
Writes the family chronicle for each decade. The numbers are deterministic and identical for every table, so the competition is fair and the lesson is repeatable.
The table
Argues each of the five decisions, then lives with the compounding.
Time
Four minutes a decade, five on the scoreboard. Short version: 1995, 2005, 2035 and 2045.
Three Generations, in play
What they leave with

Fortunes leak; they rarely crash. The proverb is arithmetic.

Governance looks like a cost until the 2035 tax decade prices it.

Trust is on the balance sheet, whatever the accountants say.

The advisor's moment
"Which decision felt cautious and turned out expensive?"
Watch for
Tables treating governance as a cost. Let the tax decade make the argument.
The Call
15 min
Sunday, 16:31. A message from your father's new assistant.

A simulated impersonation scam, built from things that have happened to real families in the last two years. The table plays one phone and decides together when to stop.

The security game: how it flows
1
The brief
Two minutes. Your father is a public figure; his voice is on the internet. Your phone buzzes.
2
Mira writes
A warm, urgent, plausible assistant. Three replies to choose from, or type your own.
3
It escalates
The last four digits. A voice note from "Dad". A six-digit code. A secure link.
4
The exit
Call Dad on his usual number at any moment. Or approve the transfer. Nobody says which.
5
Debrief
What was given away, the seven tactics used, the three habits that end it.
6
Ask the fraudster
The AI explains, as the person behind Mira, how each reply was read on the other side.
The AI
Plays Mira whenever the table types its own reply, and explains the playbook afterwards. The core path is scripted so every table meets the same tactics.
The table
Chooses every reply together, on one phone, under a deadline.
Time
Two minutes to brief, eight on the phone, five to debrief. Play it twice if there is time.
The Call, in play
What they leave with

A family safe word, agreed tonight.

Never read a code to anyone. The bank never asks.

Money moves one way, slowly, through a process agreed in daylight.

The advisor's moment
"At which message did you first feel uneasy? Why did you keep going?"
Watch for
Who wants to call, and who talks them out of it. Do not help, not even with a look.
The Investment Committee
20 min
A founder wants €5 million. You get ten questions.

Three founders, each with a polished pitch and four things they will only tell you if you ask precisely. The committee questions, votes, and finds out five years later.

The diligence game: how it flows
1
Choose a founder
Cartelle, Ledgerly or Nomad Clinics. Each asks €5 million; each hides something different.
2
The pitch
Four slides: problem, product, traction, ask. Confident numbers, all technically true.
3
Ten questions
Ten tokens. The founder answers in character, volunteers nothing, and never lies.
4
Vote
Each person commits real money from the family pot: pass, €0.5m, €1m or €2m. Out loud, then tap.
5
Five years later
What happened, what came back, and a diligence score: how many of the hidden facts the table found.
6
What was there to find
The four facts, ticked or missed. The founder never lied; the committee never asked.
The AI
Plays the founder. Discloses a hidden truth only when a question touches it precisely; otherwise answers with something true and flattering.
The table
Asks, votes, and compares diligence scores with the other tables.
Time
Three minutes on the pitch, ten on questions, two to vote, five on the reveal.
The Investment Committee, in play
What they leave with

The quality of the question is the whole job.

Churn, margin, contracts, licences, and who actually built it.

A founder who never lies is not the same as a founder who tells you.

The advisor's moment
"Which of the missed facts would have changed your vote?"
Watch for
Questions about vision and market. Nudge towards numbers.
The Grant Round
20 min
€10 million to give, and nobody to audit you.

The foundation board has three dossiers: a famous brand with no evidence, a boring data trust that works, and a 27-year-old with one boat and a government match waiting.

The philanthropy game: how it flows
1
The board brief
€10 million this year, for oceans and education. Three applicants have written.
2
The dossiers
Folder tabs. Each applicant: the pitch, three facts, and four questions the board can ask.
3
Four questions each
The applicant answers in character. The polished one is hollow if you ask about outcomes; the quiet one cannot absorb more than €1 million a year.
4
Allocate
Half-million steps, up to €10 million. Anything unallocated is held.
5
Stamp it
Funded or declined, in red.
6
Three years later
What each grant did, an impact score, and a verdict on where the money went.
The AI
Plays each applicant with hidden truths: no evaluation in six years; 62% on communications; a founder who retires with a named successor; a 40% chance of failure and a 1:1 government match.
The table
Questions, allocates, and defends the allocation out loud before tapping.
Time
Two minutes on the brief, ten on the dossiers, four to allocate, four on the reveal.
The Grant Round, in play
What they leave with

Polish and evidence are different things, and polish gets funded.

"How much can you usefully spend?" is the question nobody asks.

Being first money is sometimes the whole point.

The advisor's moment
"Which applicant did you like before you asked anything? Did the questions change it?"
Watch for
The word "brand".
The Values Auction
20 min
Bid for what you believe. Then find out what you would pay for.

Eight values under the hammer, €1,000 a paddle. Then, for every lot sold, one situation and two doors. The gap between the bid and the door is the conversation.

The opener: how it flows
1
Paddles
Every player gets a paddle and €1,000, fictional and final.
2
Eight lots
Privacy, Adventure, Legacy, Control, Freedom, Impact, Recognition, Belonging. Tap to bid €50 more.
3
Going once, going twice
The advisor runs the hammer. A big yellow SOLD lands on the lot. Unsold lots matter too.
4
The ledger
Who bought what, and for how much. Who has money left.
5
The test
For each lot sold, the buyer faces a situation with two doors. One costs something for the value; one does not.
6
The receipt
Held, or gap. Then the AI writes the table's values portrait.
The AI
Writes a short values portrait of the table from the ledger and the doors. The bidding and the tests are deterministic.
The table
Bids, answers, and notices who kept their money.
Time
Two minutes a lot, five for the test and the receipt. The best opener for the evening.
The Values Auction, in play
What they leave with

People buy the values they would like to have.

The doors show which ones they already pay for.

The lots nobody wanted say as much as the ones that sold.

The advisor's moment
"Which door was hardest to walk through?"
Watch for
Money left over at the end, and why.
The Cousins' Buyout
25 min
Two tables. One company. One branch wants out.

Branch A owns 30% and wants a clean exit; Branch B owns 70% and wants to keep the company. Each table has a private brief with one secret the other side would pay to know.

The negotiation game: how it flows
1
Pick a side
Each table plays a branch. The company facts are shared: €1.4bn revenue, €140m EBITDA, €200m net debt, peers at 8x.
2
The private brief
iPad turned away. A wants €270m and needs €80m in cash within a year. B can borrow €150m, and had an unsolicited €1.1bn offer for the whole company last week.
3
Negotiate
Face to face, across the tables. The iPad keeps the offer record and the twenty-minute clock.
4
Ask the valuer, ask the mediator
The AI valuer gives both sides the same range. The AI mediator proposes a bridge from the last two offers, never seeing the secrets.
5
Sign, or walk away
Cash on signing, deferred, years, a share of any future sale. Deal or no deal.
6
The verdict
Each table sees its own outcome, the other side's outcome, and the other side's private brief.
The AI
The valuer and the mediator. Same valuation to both tables; bridging structures built only from the offers on the record.
The table
Negotiates in the room, records offers, decides when to stop.
Time
Three minutes on the briefs, twenty on the clock, two to sign, then the verdict. One table can play alone against the mediator.
The Cousins' Buyout, in play
What they leave with

Information beats valuation.

"Has anyone made an offer recently?" is worth €100 million.

Know your walkaway before the clock starts.

The advisor's moment
"Was the price the real argument?"
Watch for
Nobody asking about recent offers, on either table.
Dinner with the Founder
20 min
He died in 2009. The AI has read his letters.

Henri Valmont founded the company in 1958 with a second-hand press. Tonight he is at the table, built from three letters, and the family has a decision to put in front of him.

The closer: how it flows
1
The letters
Three excerpts: to his son in 1979, to the bank in 1991, to his granddaughter in 2004. Read one aloud.
2
Sit down with him
Eight questions. A candle burns down as they go. His answers arrive slowly, like typing.
3
The decision
Sell the plastics division, bring in an outside chief executive, move the headquarters, or something of the table's own.
4
"What would you do, Henri?"
His view, with one reason from his life and one thing he admits he cannot know.
5
Follow him, or break with him
One person says the decision out loud, with the reason, before anyone taps.
6
His last word
Generous either way, and something to carry out of the room.
The AI
Plays Henri from the letters: direct, warm, occasionally wrong, and willing to disagree. Never breaks character.
The table
Asks the questions it could not ask anyone alive, then decides anyway.
Time
Four minutes on the letters, ten at dinner, six on the decision. Dim the lights.
Dinner with the Founder, in play
What they leave with

The past has a vote, not a veto.

Henri's three questions: can you pay for it yourselves, will the workers recognise it, could you explain it to my father?

Deciding out loud, with a reason, is the exercise.

The advisor's moment
"Who at your real table plays Henri?"
Watch for
Eight polite questions. Push for the one they would not ask a living person.

Running it well

The advisor's job is four things.

Every game

Set the table. Names in the app before guests sit down. Decide who plays what before anyone can decline.

Keep the clock. The app has one for every game. Start it out loud. Stop the table when it rings, even mid-sentence.

Ask the second question. The first answer is the polite one. The Facilitator drawer has the second question for every game.

Capture what changed. Stamps, receipts, verdicts, chronicles. Read one back at the end of the evening.

When it goes wrong

The AI is slow or unreachable. Every AI action falls back to sample output and says so. Nothing stops. Switch on demo mode if it keeps happening.

Somebody typed something real. Facilitator drawer, New table. It clears everything on that iPad.

A table is stuck. Spin for who speaks. Throw a curveball. Ask the AI to argue the empty seat. Move to the debrief early rather than late.

Two tables, uneven numbers. The Buyout can be played alone against the mediator; Three Generations does not care how many tables post.

Before the first evening

A short list.

1
Rehearse once
Open the app on each iPad, switch on demo mode, and play one game start to finish. Fifteen minutes. The app behaves exactly the same live.
2
Set the room
iPads charged and named. A screen for the scoreboard and for this deck. Two tables within earshot if the Buyout is on. Lights that can go down for the Founder.
3
Read the drawers
Each game's Facilitator drawer has the point, the questions and the timing on one screen. Read the ones you will run. Do not read them aloud.

Then say the rule, once, before the first game: everything on the iPad is fiction, and the table decides, never the AI.

Nobody wins any of these games.Everyone leaves with something real: a question they cannot unhear, a habit, a number, a name.
